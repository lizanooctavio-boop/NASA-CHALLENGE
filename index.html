            How a trend detective decides

            The ideas behind every verdict in this app.

            What is a trend?

            A trend is a change that keeps going in one direction over many years: rising or falling, wetter or drier, warmer or cooler. Single years jump up and down for many reasons, so a detective looks at the whole record and asks whether the long-term slope stands out from that year-to-year noise.

            We report the slope as change per decade, in the same units as the data, so “+0.30 °C per decade” means about three tenths of a degree warmer every ten years.

            Confidence interval

            The slope is an estimate. The 95% confidence interval is the range of slopes that are consistent with the data: if we could repeat the measurement many times, about 95 out of 100 intervals built this way would contain the true value. A narrow interval means a precise estimate; a wide one means a lot of uncertainty.

            If the interval includes zero, the data cannot rule out “no change”.

            p-value

            The p-value answers one question: if there were no trend at all, how often would random year-to-year variation produce a pattern at least this strong? A small p-value (below 0.05 here) means that would rarely happen by chance.

            It is not the probability that the trend is real, and a small p-value does not prove the cause of a change. It also says nothing about whether the change is large enough to matter: always read it next to the size of the slope.

            “Not significant” is not “no change”

            When the verdict is “Not enough evidence of a trend” (or “Insufficient evidence”), a change may still be happening — the available record simply cannot yet separate it from natural year-to-year swings. A short record or large year-to-year swings often hide real long-term shifts (statisticians call missing a real change a Type II error).

            A longer record can make the same change detectable. That is why a trend that falls short of significance is still worth reporting.

            Why months are not independent

            A wet month is often followed by another wet month, and every year repeats the same seasonal cycle. Tests that treat each month as an independent sample count the same information many times and become overconfident.

            Example from this app: chlorophyll in the Gulf of Fonseca. A straight line through 264 raw monthly values gives p = 0.0007. After removing the seasonal cycle, averaging each year and correcting for autocorrelation, the Mann–Kendall test gives p = 0.08: the evidence is much weaker than it first looked.

            Three methods, one verdict

            Every series is tested three ways at the 95% confidence level (two-sided α = 0.05): a least-squares line with an interval corrected for autocorrelation, the same line with Newey–West (HAC) errors, and the non-parametric Theil–Sen slope, which is robust to unusual years. The Mann–Kendall test, corrected for autocorrelation, checks whether values tend to rise or fall without assuming a straight line.

            • Trend detected: all three intervals exclude zero with the same sign and both tests agree.
            • Not enough evidence of a trend: all three intervals include zero (which still does not rule out a real change hidden by variability).
            • Methods disagree: at least one test or confidence interval rules out zero, while another still includes zero or gives p ≥ 0.05 (for example, when extreme years pull one estimator past the threshold but not another). Even when two slopes look similar and point in the same direction, the verdict depends on the method chosen, so it is not presented as a confirmed trend.

            Detection is not attribution. A “Trend detected” verdict establishes only a consistent directional change in the observed series; it does not identify why the series changed (climate forcing, multi-decadal variability, water management, land-use change or observing-system shifts). Where published process or attribution studies exist, they are cited separately in the notes.

            Many questions, more false alarms

            Each test has about a 5% chance of a false alarm. Test many variables and places and some will look significant just by luck. Maps of many grid cells use the false discovery rate correction to keep that in check, and a single significant result is always read next to its cross-checks.

            Short records

            Some satellites are new. SWOT has measured lake levels only since 2023, so its record shows the seasons clearly but is far too short for a trend test. We use it to confirm an older, longer record instead.

            The 2015–2019 monthly SMAP record (57 observations) shows how wet Nicaragua’s soils get through the seasons, but 5 years is far too short for a multi-decade trend test. Investigate also includes longer GLDAS records (since 2001): SMAP estimates moisture in the top 5 cm of soil, whereas GLDAS averages the top 10 cm. Local soil measurements would be needed to validate either estimate.

            Synthetic-aperture radar (SAR) snapshots from Sentinel-1 and the RADARSAT Constellation Mission (RCM) illustrate how open-water area is mapped through clouds on Lake Cocibolca. Because each pass covers only part of the lake and lacks local ground validation, these few scenes cannot support a trend test.

            Other trend tests

            Seasonal Mann–Kendall. In monthly data the seasonal cycle swamps a slow trend, so the ordinary Mann–Kendall test loses sensitivity. The seasonal version compares each month only with the same month in other years. This app reaches the same goal by testing annual totals and season totals instead of raw months.

            Unit-root tests (augmented Dickey–Fuller). They ask a different question: whether a series wanders like a random walk, not whether it rises or falls. A series with a clear trend can pass or fail them, so they are not used here to decide whether a trend exists.

            Based on Statistical test methods to detect trends in time series data (MCMC Addict, 2024).

            Record years, El Niño and how it rains

            One extreme year is not a trend. In 2020 hurricanes Eta and Iota hit Nicaragua two weeks apart; in 2026, a strong El Niño year, the Pacific lowlands had their driest January–September since 1981. Swings like these, driven largely by El Niño and La Niña, are much larger than a slow change in the yearly total, so they can hide it. Removing the part that follows the El Niño index is one way to look underneath.

            The total is also only one way to describe rain. The same yearly amount can fall on fewer, heavier days or on more, lighter ones, and dry spells can grow inside the rainy season. That is why this app also tests how many days bring heavy rain, how hard it rains on a wet day, the wettest five days and the longest dry spell.

            Finally, the data source itself can change. A reanalysis such as MERRA-2 suggests Nicaragua’s rainfall doubled since the 1980s, while satellites and rain gauges show nothing of the kind: the observations fed into the model changed, not the climate. Main rainfall verdicts here therefore rely on observation-based products (such as IMERG, CHIRPS and CRU) rather than unconstrained reanalyses like MERRA-2; even so, blended gauge–satellite grids can shift as station counts change over time, so full-record rainfall trends should be checked against recent decades and independent sources.

            Cross-checks

            Every dataset has its own errors. A satellite can confuse sediment with algae; a weather station can move; a reanalysis can jump when new observations are added. When a second source or a second method reaches the same verdict, the conclusion is more trustworthy. When they disagree, the disagreement itself is a clue worth investigating.
